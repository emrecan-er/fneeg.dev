Camera, made simple

iPhone 18 Pro variable aperture, explained without camera jargon.

The new camera feature changes how much light enters and how much of a scene appears in focus. Here is when that actually matters.

By Emrecan ErOctober 11, 20264 min read

The iPhone 18 Pro and Pro Max add a variable aperture to their 48MP Fusion Main camera. In ordinary language, aperture is the opening that lets light into a camera. A wider opening lets in more light and tends to make the background softer. A narrower opening lets in less light but can keep more of a scene looking sharp. The new part is that the opening can physically change.

Apple says the camera adjusts it automatically for light and depth of field, and lets you choose among four settings in the Camera app. That means you can leave it alone most of the time, then take control when the result needs a nudge. The details come from Apple's iPhone 18 Pro announcement.

Why group photos are a useful test

In a group photo, people often stand at different distances from the lens. A wider aperture can favor the front row and leave faces farther back looking softer. A narrower aperture can help keep more of the group in focus. Apple specifically calls out crisper detail toward the back of group photos as a benefit of the new camera.

Try a simple comparison in good light: take one photo with the automatic setting, then another using a narrower aperture. Look at faces near the front and back, not only the person in the center. If the automatic shot already works, you have learned that you can trust it for that scene.

What changes after dark?

In lower light, a wider opening gives the camera more light to work with. The tradeoff is that less of the scene may appear sharply focused. If you are photographing one person in a dim café, that may be exactly what you want. For a group at the same table, pay attention to whether the people at the edges or farther back still look clear.

Aperture is only one part of an image. Movement, focus, exposure, and Apple's image processing still affect the result. The new control does not make every night photo perfect; it gives you another way to respond when a photo is too dark or when the background is softer than you intended.

Should you use manual mode?

Use the automatic setting for quick moments. Reach for a manual aperture when you have a few extra seconds and a specific goal: more of a group in focus, or more separation between a subject and the background. Take two versions and compare them at full size. You will learn more from your own light and distance than from memorizing an aperture number.

A practical rule: first decide what must be sharp. If it is one face, a wider opening can be useful. If it is several people at different distances, try a narrower one.

What happens after the shot?

The value of a new camera is the photos you want to keep, not more photos to sort. If testing settings leaves you with five versions of every scene, choose your favorite soon after the moment. Our photo cleanup guide has a calm routine for that last step.
